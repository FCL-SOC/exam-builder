## Writing good questions

Write for the students in front of you: assess what has been taught, in language suited to the year level, using
Australian spelling and SI units (with a space: 25 m, 3.5 kg, 20 °C).

## Command terms

Start each question or part with one command term, used as VCE students learn it (the VCAA glossary of command
terms). The marks in brackets are the usual range: set them by how many distinct points a full answer needs.

- Name, State, Identify (1 mark): give a word, fact or feature, without explanation.
- Define (1–2 marks): give the precise meaning of a term.
- List (1 mark per item): give several items, no detail.
- Label (1 mark per 2–3 labels): add names to parts of a diagram.
- Outline (2 marks): give the main points briefly.
- Describe (2–3 marks): give the features or characteristics of something, or what happens.
- Calculate, Determine (1–4 marks): work out a value, showing working and units.
- Sketch (2–4 marks): draw the shape and key features (intercepts, turning points, labels), not to exact scale.
- Interpret (2–3 marks): say what data, a graph or a source shows.
- Distinguish (2 marks): give the difference(s) between two things.
- Explain (2–4 marks): give how or why: a point, and the reason or cause linking it.
- Show that (2–3 marks): prove a given result step by step (used when a later part needs the result).
- Apply (2–3 marks): use knowledge or a rule in a new situation.
- Predict, Suggest (1–2 marks): give a likely outcome or idea, with a reason.
- Compare (2–4 marks): give similarities AND differences, both sides each time.
- Analyse (3–4 marks): break into parts and show how they relate or what causes what.
- Justify (2–3 marks): support a decision or answer with evidence or reasons.
- Discuss (4–6 marks): consider different sides or factors, reaching a view.
- Evaluate (4–6 marks): weigh strengths and limitations against criteria, then make a judgement.

Use one command term per part. If a question asks for two things ("Identify … and explain …"), split it into parts
a and b, each with its own marks.

## Marks

- A mark is one distinct, creditable point or step. A 3-mark "Explain" needs three things to credit.
- Calculations: about one mark per key step, and the last mark for the final answer with units and the rounding
  asked for. Say how to round: "Give your answer correct to two decimal places."
- Allow roughly one mark per minute of writing time (a little less for Years 7–8).
- Spread the demand: about a quarter recall (Name, State, Define), half understanding and application (Describe,
  Explain, Calculate), a quarter higher-order (Compare, Analyse, Evaluate). Start each section and each multi-part
  question with the easier part.
- When a later part depends on an earlier answer, say so: "Hence …" or "Using your answer to part a …", so a wrong
  earlier answer can be followed through.

## Answer space

Lay answer space out as a VCAA question and answer book does.

- Every written answer, including the working for a calculation, goes on ruled lines (a lines block). Don't use an
  empty box for writing.
- Three lines per mark, and never fewer than three lines. So 1 mark: 3 lines, 2 marks: 6 lines, 3 marks: 9 lines,
  4 marks: 12 lines. Calculations need about four lines per mark. Students' handwriting is bigger than you think.
- Mathematics (any year, General, Methods and Specialist): lines only. No answer boxes and no working boxes; the
  student writes the final answer on the last line.
- Physics and Chemistry calculations: lines for the working, then one answer box with the units printed in it.
- Sketches and graphs: blank axes (a graph) with sensible scales. Use a box only for a diagram drawn without axes.
- Put the answer space straight after the part's wording, in the same order under every part.
- Multiple choice needs no extra space.

## Multiple choice

- Four options (A–D), exactly one clearly correct, each worth 1 mark.
- Wrong options should be plausible: the answers students get from common mistakes or misconceptions.
- Keep options parallel in grammar and similar in length; put numbers in order.
- The stem is a complete question, or a sentence the options complete. Put shared words in the stem, not the options.
- Avoid "all of the above", "none of the above" and double negatives. If a negative is essential, write it in
  capitals: "Which of the following is NOT …".
- Always mark the correct option (it is shown to the teacher, never printed).

## Wording

- Give every number, unit and condition a student needs, in the stem; nothing should depend on guessing what the
  writer meant.
- Use short sentences and everyday words; explain any context a student might not know.
- Contexts should be realistic and inclusive: a mix of names, cultures and situations, nothing that disadvantages
  some students.
- No trick questions. Difficulty should come from the content, not the wording.
- Don't write question numbers, part letters or marks in the text; the exam adds them.

## Structure

- A usual order is Section A multiple choice, then Section B short answer, then any extended response.
- Group parts under one question when they share a stimulus (a table, graph, source or scenario); put the stimulus
  in the question, before part a.
